# Polaris 14-Day Customer Discovery Sprint

Prepared: 2026-07-23

## Objective

Run a two-week discovery exercise in smart-telescope communities to validate
the problem Polaris should solve. This is not an advertising campaign. The
early objective is for users to describe the planning problem in their own
words—not to make them recognize or want Project Polaris by name.

Use two or three strong groups. Stagger posts every two or three days, vary the
wording slightly, and review each group's rules for surveys, promotion, beta
testing, and external links before posting.

## Evidence tracker

For every useful response, record:

- User type: beginner, intermediate, or experienced.
- Telescope: Dwarf 3, Seestar, Vespera, or other.
- Problem described.
- Current workaround.
- Frequency and severity.
- Requested solution, if offered without prompting.
- Willingness to beta test.
- Willingness to pay, only when volunteered.
- Critical-path classification: must-have, useful, or distraction.

Also distinguish whether the user's primary interest is finding targets,
capturing targets, processing images, or sharing results. Polaris should not
attempt to solve all four during beta.

## Schedule

### Day 1 — Observe without posting

Review recent discussions for phrases and themes such as:

- What should I image?
- What targets tonight?
- Best settings.
- Clouds, Moon, and light pollution.
- Can't find, framing, and processing.
- New user.
- Wish the app.

Save screenshots of recurring complaints. Do not mention Polaris.

### Day 2 — Learn the actual workflow

Ask:

> For those of you using a Dwarf 3 or another smart telescope, how do you
> decide what you're going to image on a particular night?
>
> Do you use an astronomy app, Facebook recommendations, a target list, weather
> apps, or just open the telescope app and look around?
>
> I'm especially curious how many different apps or websites you normally
> check before starting.

Useful follow-ups:

- Which apps do you use?
- What information do you check in each?
- How long does that take?
- Which part is most frustrating?
- What happens when you choose the wrong target?

Do not ask what features they want yet. Collect responses after 24–48 hours.

### Day 4 — Identify the largest planning frustration

Ask:

> What is the single most frustrating part of planning a night with your smart
> telescope?
>
> Not processing afterward—the part between deciding you might go outside and
> actually starting the capture.
>
> For example: weather uncertainty, picking a target, knowing whether the Moon
> will interfere, finding the right start time, light pollution, framing,
> settings, or something else.
>
> What slows you down the most?

Let users disagree. Do not defend a proposed solution. Give extra weight to
repeated emotional language such as “I hate,” “I always struggle,” “I wish,”
“It takes forever,” “I never know,” and “I keep making this mistake.”

### Day 6 — Poll the hardest decision

Use the group's native poll feature:

> What is the hardest part of deciding what to image tonight?

Choices:

- Knowing which targets are visible.
- Weather and cloud uncertainty.
- Moon brightness or position.
- Knowing the best time to begin.
- Choosing targets for my light pollution.
- Knowing whether the target fits my telescope.
- Choosing capture settings.
- I usually already know what I want.
- Other—explain in comments.

Do not include “all-in-one app”; it would lead respondents.

### Day 8 — Test the central Polaris problem

Ask:

> Would this actually be useful, or would it be overkill?
>
> You enter your location and telescope, and it gives you a short list of the
> best realistic targets for that night based on visibility, weather, Moon
> conditions, light pollution, framing, and how much time you have.
>
> Instead of showing hundreds of possible objects, it might say:
>
> “Here are your best three choices tonight, why each one is worth capturing,
> and the best time to begin.”
>
> Would you use something like that regularly? What would it absolutely need to
> get right for you to trust it?

Track trust requirements: accuracy, weather sources, horizons and obstructions,
equipment-specific framing, target altitude, Moon separation, user skill,
recommended settings, capture history, notifications, and simplicity. Preserve
negative responses.

### Day 10 — Force-rank the main information

Ask:

> You can only see five pieces of information before deciding whether to
> capture a target. Which five matter most?

Offer examples:

- Target altitude.
- Visibility window.
- Moon separation.
- Cloud forecast.
- Wind.
- Light-pollution suitability.
- Telescope framing.
- Recommended exposure settings.
- Example image.
- Difficulty level.
- Total capture time.
- Best start time.
- Whether the user captured it previously.

Ask users to rank their five. Use results to separate the main recommendation
screen from deeper detail.

### Day 12 — Examine the last failed session

Ask:

> Think about the last time you planned to use your telescope but didn't—or you
> started and gave up. What happened?

Look for setup friction, batteries, Wi-Fi, dew, poor alignment, processing
intimidation, lack of time, or uncertainty that the result would be worthwhile.
Identify what would actually have prevented the failed session.

### Day 14 — Reveal the project carefully

Post:

> I've appreciated everyone's answers over the last couple of weeks. I'm a
> smart-telescope user too, and I've been working on an early companion tool
> designed to make the “What should I image tonight?” decision easier.
>
> It is not ready for release, and I'm not selling anything. I'm trying to make
> sure it solves real problems instead of becoming another app filled with
> charts and features people do not use.
>
> I'll eventually need a small group of Dwarf 3 and smart-telescope users to
> test an early beta. Experience level would not matter—I'd actually want a mix
> of beginners and experienced users.
>
> Would anyone be interested in being contacted when testing opens? Also, what
> would make you excited to try it rather than ignore it?

Ask interested users to comment. Do not send them to an external signup or
payment page yet.

## Comment-interview pattern

Use this progression:

1. That's interesting. How do you handle it currently?
2. How often does that cause a problem?
3. What would a genuinely useful solution look like?
4. Would solving that change how often you use the telescope?

The final question distinguishes genuine pain from casual wishlist items.

## Guardrails

During this sprint, avoid:

- Posting screenshots of Polaris too early.
- Asking people to choose colors, logos, or names.
- Offering a long feature list.
- Promising artificial intelligence.
- Testing a price such as $4.99.
- Starting a separate Facebook group immediately.
- Posting every day.
- Copying identical posts into many groups.
- Criticizing the DwarfLab app.
- Implying an official relationship with DwarfLab.

Internal product naming may continue separately, but the discovery groups
should not be asked to validate names during this sprint.

## Sprint output

After Day 8, begin consolidating findings even if feedback is negative. At the
end of Day 14, produce:

- A structured feedback tracker.
- Evidence-backed user segments.
- Ranked problems by frequency, severity, and critical-path impact.
- A prioritized beta feature list.
- A list of trust requirements.
- A list of interested beta testers.
- Explicit recommendations for what Polaris should not build yet.
